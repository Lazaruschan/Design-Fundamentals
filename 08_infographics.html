SSHD2382 · Category 08

Information Graphics

Category 08 · Course link: Week 4 Visual Hierarchy · Main references: Hiroyuki Kimura, 《圖解力：跟頂級設計師學作信息圖》; 《以圖釋義：信息圖表設計》; 《信息圖表設計入門》 (pictogram section); 《信息圖表設計：全球創意入門》

1. Scope

This category has only 4 core books (plus 1 duplicate), but together they cover the complete framework of information graphics:

Book Focus
《圖解力》 Five types of information graphic (diagram, chart and table, graph, map, pictogram) and five key qualities
《以圖釋義》 A seven-step production process + interviews with international designers
《信息圖表設計入門》 Design steps and uses of the pictogram
《全球創意入門》 100 data visualisation cases, 8 ways of representing data

Infographic = turning information (data, structure, process, location) into visual form so that people understand it faster and more accurately. It sits between design and editing: you have to "understand" before you can "diagram".

2. Core Concepts

2.1 Five Basic Types (《圖解力》)

DiagramUses shapes and arrows to explain structure, relationships and processes. Examples: organisation charts, flowcharts, concept maps, cross-sections.
Chart & TableOrganises information in rows and columns for easy comparison. Examples: timetables, comparison tables, matrices.
GraphTurns quantities into length, area or position. Examples: bar charts, line charts, pie charts, scatter plots.
MapShows spatial location and distance. Examples: route maps, guide maps, thematic maps, railway line diagrams.
PictogramReplaces words with simplified images, crossing language barriers. Examples: toilets, emergency exits, Olympic sports symbols.

2.2 Choosing a Graph

To show Suitable forms
Comparison Bar chart, dot plot
Change over time Line chart, area chart, timeline
Composition Stacked bar chart, pie chart (fewer than five items), treemap
Distribution Histogram, box plot
Relationship Scatter plot, bubble chart, network diagram
Flow Sankey diagram, parallel sets
Location Thematic map, dot distribution map
Structure / Process Diagram, flowchart, cross-section

2.3 Five Qualities of a Good Information Graphic

《圖解力》 and 《以圖釋義》 both put forward the same set of criteria:

Quality Meaning Self-check
Attractive Makes people want to look at first glance Is there one strong visual entry point?
Clear Information is accurate and cannot be misread Are the proportions, scales and units correct?
Simple Keeps the essentials and drops the rest If you delete this element, does understanding get worse?
Flow The eye follows a clear reading order that builds time and space Do readers know where to start and where to finish?
Wordless Uses images instead of words wherever possible Can readers get the main idea without reading the captions?

2.4 Three Organising Structures

After gathering the material, first decide which "axis" to organise it along (《以圖釋義》):

  • Time: history, steps, schedules → timelines, flowcharts.
  • Space: location, structure, sections → maps, cutaway drawings, exploded views.
  • Logic: categories, cause and effect, comparison, hierarchy → matrices, tree diagrams, Venn diagrams, organisation charts.

3. Principles & Methods

3.1 Seven-Step Production Process (《以圖釋義》)

flowchart LR
  a["1 Choose a topic"] --> b["2 Gather data"]
  b --> c["3 Analyse data<br/>time, space, logic"]
  c --> d["4 Decide the form<br/>medium, material, chart type"]
  d --> e["5 Sketch"]
  e --> f["6 Finalise design"]
  f --> g["7 Test and publish"]
  g -.Feedback.-> c

《圖解力》 puts special stress on "building and rebuilding the framework": first use sketches to build a framework for understanding, then keep overturning and reorganising it until the structure is as simple as possible and as close as possible to the reader's question.

3.2 Honest Data

  • The value axis of a bar chart should start at 0; otherwise it exaggerates differences.
  • Do not use 3D effects that distort areas and angles.
  • When area represents quantity, the area (not the diameter) must be proportional to the value.
  • State the data source, date and units.
  • Colour carries meaning: keep the same data category in the same colour throughout the graphic.

3.3 Steps for Designing Pictograms (《信息圖表設計入門》)

  1. Confirm the purpose: why do you need a pictogram? Will it be used in many places, in small spaces, or across languages?
  2. Extract the features: find the most recognisable outline or action of the subject.
  3. Unify the specifications: keep line weight, corner radius, proportion and visual weight consistent across the whole icon set.
  4. Test recognition: show it at the target size to people from different backgrounds. Can they understand it without reading any words?
  5. Systematise: combine it with colour, layout and caption text to form a complete visual language.

The 1964 Tokyo Olympics were the first to use sports pictograms as a system, influencing every international event and public signage system since.

3.4 Links to Layout and Hierarchy

Information graphics are essentially an application of Week 4 visual hierarchy: title → main image → key figures → explanation → source. Accent colour, contrast of size, white space and the grid (see 04 Layout & Grid) all apply here too.

6. Study Cues

  1. Draw your route to school as a simplified map that keeps only five landmarks.
  2. Find a graph in a newspaper and check whether its value axis, units and data source are honest.
  3. Show the same set of data (for example, a week's spending) as a bar chart, a pie chart and a treemap. Which is clearest? Why?
  4. Design a set of four pictograms for a classroom (no eating or drinking, charging point, recycling, quiet), keeping lines and proportions consistent.
  5. Score an online infographic against the five qualities (attractive, clear, simple, flow, wordless).

7. Each Book

Each book in the bookshelf is described below. Every title above links to its section.

圖解力

Author: Hiroyuki Kimura

Scope. This is a book of methods for information graphics, not an annual of finished work. Hiroyuki Kimura sorts the information graphics found in newspapers, magazines, books and web pages into several types, and explains how to find a point of view that makes readers want to keep looking. Use it to set up categories before starting an infographic project.

Main ideas

  • A diagram organises information functionally; an infographic must also meet the needs of reporting, with the layout worked over again and again so that people want to keep reading.
  • The main types in the book are: Diagram, Chart and Table, Graph, Map, and Pictogram.
  • A good infographic uses metaphors that fit cultural habits, and the designer's point of view must match the reader's.
  • If a chronology-type graphic uses only one straight line, it becomes misleading as soon as the scale changes; the form must follow the nature of the data.
  • The Malofiej Awards show that the field covers both print and animated online infographics.

Methods

  • First decide which type your material belongs to (process, comparison, statistics, map, symbol), then choose the chart type. Do not start by picking an attractive template.
  • Each graphic answers only one question. Write the question in the title.
  • Check the proportions: the scales of timelines, maps and bar charts must be honest.
  • When you finish, ask: without reading the text, can readers state the conclusion of this graphic within ten seconds?

Study cues

  1. Give one example you have seen of each: diagram, chart, graph, map and pictogram.
  2. Draw a class timeline, first as an evenly spaced straight line, then in a way that shows "which period is especially long". Compare which is more honest.

以圖釋義：信息圖表設計

Scope. This book goes from "what is an infographic" to making one, and interviews a number of designers. It boils down the standard for a good graphic to five words, which makes it useful for assessing your own work.

Main ideas

  • Attractive, Clear, Simple, Flow, Wordless. Clarity means handing the information to the reader in full; simplicity means removing elements that create a burden.
  • The eye moves while reading, so design a path rather than spreading everything evenly.
  • Text that is too dense throws away the advantage of an infographic; if an image can say it, do not add another paragraph.
  • The techniques in the book include reworking bar charts, turning time into flow, creating visual stimulus, giving information a spatial form, and using scenes to act out data.

Methods

  • Score your graphic on these five words; the lowest one is what to fix in the next draft.
  • Write a one-sentence conclusion first, then choose the chart type. If one sentence cannot say it, the topic is still too big.
  • When making a bar chart, ask whether it could become "time moving" or "space unfolding" instead of just five bars.
  • When you use the designer interviews in the book, note their process, not their illustration style.

Study cues

  1. Choose an infographic you like and write one sentence each on its strengths or problems in terms of clear, simple and flow.
  2. Turn a set of five numbers into a bar chart, then into a scene with a reading order. Compare which makes the conclusion easier to remember.

信息圖表設計入門

Scope. This is an introductory book that starts from the components. The author argues that you should not begin with one large infographic, but first practise two components: pictograms and diagrams.

Main ideas

  • Infographics are mostly made of pictograms, diagrams and images. Only once you can make the components can you make the whole graphic.
  • Three basic skills should be practised together: organising information clearly, drawing it, and arranging it in a readable order.
  • A pictogram is not about drawing an object realistically, but about extracting the features that can be "recognised at a glance". The men's and women's toilet symbols are an example: they convey a convention, not a portrait.
  • The book is structured as: cases, pictograms, diagrams, infographics. Only later does it cover scenarios, key points and process.

Methods

  • This week, make just ten pictograms (some objects, some actions, some places), limited to two colours and one line weight.
  • Each diagram expresses only one relationship: sequence, comparison, or cause and effect. Do not make one graphic do three things at once.
  • Once the components are done, pick a small topic (for example, "the eight minutes before leaving home in the morning") and combine the components into one graphic.
  • Quantity-type graphics suit numerical values; confirm the figures first, then decide how much one symbol stands for.

Study cues

  1. Draw a pictogram each for "queueing, rain, rushing for a train, finding a seat" that classmates can recognise.
  2. Explain the difference between a "pictogram" and an "illustration", and say which one your drawings are.

信息圖表設計：全球創意入門

Scope. This book is built mainly around about a hundred cases and designer interviews, introducing different ways of representing information. Use it to broaden your view, not as your only step-by-step manual. As you read, ask "what reading problem does this technique solve?"

Main ideas

  • Looking good but impossible to understand is the most common failure of infographics. Complexity can stay, but readers must be able to choose whether to look for one minute or one hour.
  • The interviewees keep returning to the same points: play first, then do the rational analysis; the relationship with the client sets the pace.
  • End readers are usually smarter than designers think. There is no need to make a "for dummies" guide, but the structure must let people find a way in.
  • The cases cover guides, timelines, places and everyday data (for example, an introduction to red wine, or a record of one person's day).

Methods

  • For each case you look at, take notes in three lines: what the topic is, what the technique is, and whether any information is left if you strip away the decoration.
  • When making your own graphic, first design "a layer that can be read in one minute" and "a layer seen only by those willing to stay longer".
  • When collecting styles, keep two separate folders: structures you can borrow, and illustration surfaces you should not borrow.

Study cues

  1. Choose two works from the book and write down what each one's "one-minute layer" is.
  2. In your own next infographic, which part must be seen first, and which part can be hidden in the second layer?
